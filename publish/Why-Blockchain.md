## Historical Backdrop

As societies grew into civilizations, humans faced a challenge: how to manage resources and coordinate activities at scale. The solution was institutions - organized groups like banks or governments that enable delegation and collective decision-making.

### The Evolution of Money and Institutions

Money provides a clear example. Over time, it has taken many forms: gold and silver, iron, wheat, salt, and credit (debt). Despite these changes, money has often shifted control from individual commodities toward institutions.

Practical considerations drove this centralization. Carrying gold or silver is cumbersome for high-value transactions; iron is heavy, and wheat spoils quickly. These limitations encouraged societies to develop paper money and debt-based instruments. In Europe, private banks began issuing paper money from the 17th century onward, and in the United States, private banknotes circulated widely before the creation of the Federal Reserve in 1913.

### The Rise of Central Banking

Central banks emerged to standardize and control national money supplies. Unlike commercial banks that serve individuals and businesses, central banks - institutions like the Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, or Bank of England - manage currency issuance, set interest rates, and regulate the broader banking system.

Today, issuing money is primarily the role of central governments and central banks, standardizing currency across nearly all modern economies. Much of this money is now digital rather than physical, existing as balances in bank accounts, payment networks, and central bank reserves (the accounts banks hold at central banks).

### From Commodity to Fiat Money

Today's system runs on what's called "fiat money", currency backed not by physical commodities, but by trust in the issuing government. These evolutions (from commodity money to gold-backed paper money to fiat money to digital fiat money) all introduced their own issues, which the next stage of the evolution intended to resolve. In the current iteration, where digital fiat money exists all around the world and is tied deeply into both domestic and cross-border trade, the primary issue is due to ongoing money supply expansion. At its core, central banks around the world have (since the 1970s) issued currency **fully** backed by trust in government institutions rather than physical goods (prior to the 1970s, currencies were _partially_ backed by gold). This has been enormously helpful to governments as it effectively allows them to spend more than they collect in taxes (an outcome of policy decisions).

### The Consequences of Money Supply Expansion

The impact over time has been growth in the price of assets. When central banks create more money, each unit becomes less valuable, driving up prices of limited assets like housing, equity (stocks), and commodities like gold and silver. As essential assets like homes become less affordable while money itself loses purchasing power, this contributes to declining levels of trust in institutions. 

Against this backdrop, Bitcoin emerged, not as a replacement for fiat currency, but as an alternative designed to reduce reliance on any single monetary system.

Unlike traditional money, which relies on centralized institutions for issuance and verification, Bitcoin (for the first time) allowed value to be transferred and verified without a central authority, creating a new, _decentralized_ way to store and move value. Bitcoin serves as the clearest example of how blockchain technology addresses these centralization risks, by exploring how it works, we can understand the broader principles that make blockchain valuable beyond just money.

## What is Bitcoin / What is Decentralization?

To understand how blockchain works, let's examine Bitcoin. While Bitcoin specifically addresses money, the principles it demonstrates (decentralization, transparency, and resistance to capture) apply to blockchain technology more broadly.
### The Risk of Institutional Capture

The fundamental challenge with institutional systems isn't just about money, it's about the risk of control and capture. When critical systems are managed by centralized institutions, those institutions can become targets for influence and manipulation. Regulatory agencies might be staffed with industry insiders. Lending systems could favor politically connected borrowers. Central banks face pressure to fund government spending or bail out favored entities. Safeguards and oversight exist, but they also rely on people and institutions, creating similar vulnerabilities. Even when institutions function well, this persistent risk exists and can erode public trust.

Greece's 2015 financial crisis offers a concrete example. When banks faced collapse, the government limited ATM withdrawals to €60 per day - citizens couldn't access their own money. Whether or not this was necessary to prevent a broader crisis, it illustrates how centralized financial infrastructure creates chokepoints that can be activated when institutions come under pressure.
### How Decentralization Changes the Risk Profile

This is where decentralization offers a different approach. Capturing a centralized institution means influencing a handful of key decision-makers. Decentralized systems distribute control across many independent participants worldwide, making it far harder for any political actor to manipulate the system - you'd need to influence not five board members or 10 representatives, but rather thousands of participants. 

The rules are written in transparent, publicly inspectable code rather than decided behind closed doors, and any attempt to change them is visible to all. This doesn't eliminate all risks - decentralized systems have their own challenges including governance inefficiencies and the potential for wealth concentration - but it fundamentally changes the nature of the risks involved.

#### The Power of Exiting

Crucially, even if a majority pushes through an unpopular change, dissenting participants aren't trapped. If any portion of the network, whether 40% or 4%, disagrees strongly enough with a decision, they can choose to continue running their version of the system, essentially creating a parallel version running a different rule-set. This is fundamentally different from traditional institutions where you're stuck with whatever decisions the leadership makes. With decentralized systems, you always have an exit: if the system becomes captured or moves in a direction you oppose, you can take your participation elsewhere, preserving the version of the rules you believe in.

### Bitcoin's Implementation

Bitcoin applies this principle to money. Rather than relying on a central bank that can expand the money supply for political reasons, Bitcoin uses a decentralized network of computers running identical software. This network maintains a shared ledger (called a blockchain) that records every transaction. Think of it as a public accounting book that everyone can see and verify. To change this ledger, you'd need to convince thousands of participants running the network to adopt your changes. 

The rules are written into the code: only 21 million bitcoins will ever exist, created on a predictable schedule. No government, corporation, individual, or even a large minority of participants can unilaterally alter this. The system operates according to fixed mathematical rules rather than institutional discretion, creating money that remains neutral regardless of political pressures. 

This explanation of Bitcoin's blockchain demonstrates the core mechanism that all blockchain systems share, regardless of what they're used for.

### Creating Competitive Pressure

Importantly, blockchain-based systems don't need to replace traditional institutions to have impact. The mere existence of decentralized alternatives creates competitive pressure. When people have the option to exit to blockchain-based systems, institutions that become too captured or misaligned with user interests risk losing their "product appeal", giving them incentive to remain responsive to the people they serve. All that being said, Bitcoin only addresses one specific domain: money. What about lending, insurance, property rights, voting, governance and the countless other institutional functions that could benefit from this same decentralization? This question led to the development of smart contract blockchains.

## Beyond Money

Smart contract blockchains take the decentralization principles we've explored through Bitcoin - the distributed ledger, the transparent code, the resistance to capture, and extend them to any program, not just currency. The result is infrastructure where anyone can build services on top of services, without asking permission, and where those services can work together in ways their original creators never planned.
